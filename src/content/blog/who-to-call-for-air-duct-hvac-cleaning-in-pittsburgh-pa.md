## When do you call a duct cleaning company versus an HVAC technician?

Call a duct cleaning company when the problem is inside the ductwork itself: dust buildup, debris blowing from registers, or contamination the air handler is pushing through the house. Call an HVAC technician instead when the equipment itself is malfunctioning, that's a mechanical repair, not a cleaning job.

A lot of Pittsburgh's housing stock is older, with forced-air systems retrofitted into homes that originally ran on radiators or gravity furnaces. Ductwork in these houses often runs through joist bays and old chases that were never designed for it, which means more seams, more places for dust to collect, and more register covers that don't seal tightly. If you're seeing a visible layer of dust kicked out when the system cycles on, or it's been years since anyone's looked at the supply and return lines, that's a duct cleaning call.

If the furnace won't turn on, the breaker keeps tripping, or the thermostat isn't communicating with the unit, that's an HVAC or electrical issue, not something duct cleaning fixes. And if soot or smoke odor got pulled into the system after a fire, even a small one, that's a different scope entirely: the ducts need to be assessed and decontaminated as part of a documented [smoke damage restoration](/services/smoke-damage-restoration/) job, not a routine cleaning. Katofsky Construction handles both the duct side and the broader fire and smoke scope under one roof, which matters because insurers want to see the whole job documented together, not split across two invoices from two different companies.

## What happens when you call Katofsky Construction for duct cleaning in Pittsburgh?

The first call covers four things: the type of system (forced-air, heat pump, or a combination), roughly how many registers, when it was last cleaned (if ever), and the reason you're calling, routine maintenance, post-renovation dust, or smoke contamination from a fire. Have that information ready and the quote process goes faster.

On site, the crew inspects the supply and return registers, the main trunk lines, and the air handler itself before anything is quoted. This matters because a system with a cracked duct run or a disconnected flex line needs a different conversation than one that's just dusty. Following the process outlined by the [National Air Duct Cleaners Association](https://nadca.com/), the crew puts the system under negative pressure, agitates buildup inside the trunk and branch lines with a rotary brush, and pulls everything out with a HEPA vacuum so debris doesn't just get stirred up and resettle in the house. Registers get cleaned and resealed, and most jobs are a single-day visit for a typical single-family home.

If the job started because of a fire or a water event that pulled contamination into the HVAC system, the crew documents the ductwork separately with photos before cleaning starts, since that paperwork is what supports a restoration claim. The [EPA's indoor air quality guidance](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq) is a useful reference if you want to understand why ductwork condition affects what you're breathing day to day, particularly in a home that's had recent renovation dust or smoke exposure.
