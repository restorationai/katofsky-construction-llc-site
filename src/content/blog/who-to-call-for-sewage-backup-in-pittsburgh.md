A sewage backup in a Pittsburgh basement usually means one of three different parties needs to get involved, not just one. If you're standing over a flooded basement floor drain right now trying to figure out who picks up the phone first, here's the actual breakdown.

## Who do you call first when sewage is backing up?

Call a licensed plumber or sewer and drain contractor first if the backup is still active and coming from your own line. They have the camera and the auger or hydro-jetting equipment to find and clear a blockage in your home's lateral line, the pipe that runs from your house to the street.

If the water keeps rising after the line is cleared, or if the smell and standing water have already soaked into flooring, drywall, or a finished basement, that's when a restoration and cleanup crew takes over. Katofsky Construction handles that part: pumping out standing water, removing contaminated porous materials, and disinfecting the space to the ANSI/[IICRC S500](https://iicrc.org/s500/) standard for contaminated water losses.

Sewage backup, including any backup from a floor drain, toilet, or basement fixture that comes from beyond the trap, is classified as Category 3 water, also called black water. It's grossly contaminated and treated differently than a clean supply-line leak: more materials get removed rather than dried in place, and the crew works in full personal protective equipment.

## Is this your line, a city line, or a storm problem?

It's your responsibility if the blockage is in the lateral line between your house and the public main, the section most homeowners own even though it runs under the street right-of-way in parts of Pittsburgh. A licensed plumber or drain contractor diagnoses that.

If the blockage turns out to be in the public main itself, that's typically a matter for the Pittsburgh Water and Sewer Authority (PWSA) inside city limits, or the local municipal sewer authority in suburbs like Penn Hills, Monroeville, or McKeesport. A plumber's camera inspection usually settles which side of the property line the clog sits on.

Heavy rain adds a third factor that's specific to older parts of Pittsburgh: much of the city still runs on a combined sewer system, where stormwater and sewage share the same pipes. During heavy rain, those pipes can fill faster than they drain, and basements on the lower end of a block are often the first place the backup shows up, frequently through a floor drain rather than a fixture. The Allegheny County Sanitary Authority (ALCOSAN) manages the regional treatment system behind this, but a backup showing up in your basement during or after a storm is still a cleanup job for a restoration crew even when the root cause is system capacity, not your plumbing.

## Is it safe to stay in the house with a sewage backup?

It's not safe to spend time in a room with standing sewage, and it's not safe to let kids or pets near it at all. Category 3 water carries bacteria, viruses, and parasites that can cause illness through skin contact, inhalation, or contaminated surfaces.

Close the door to the affected area, shut off the ventilation system serving that space if you can, and avoid walking through the water. The [CDC's guidance on cleaning safely after a disaster](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/safety/index.html) covers the same basics: don't let contaminated water contact open cuts, wash hands thoroughly after any contact, and don't try to bleach-and-mop a sewage spill yourself. Our [sewage backup health risks post](/blog/sewage-backup-health-risks/) walks through why DIY cleanup of this category of water is a bad idea even with gloves and bleach on hand.

## What happens when you call a cleanup crew?

The first call is mostly information gathering: where the water is, how deep, how long it's been sitting, and whether the source has already been stopped. Have that ready along with your address and, if you've already called a plumber, what they found.

On arrival, the crew assesses the extent of contamination, sets up containment to keep the affected area isolated from the rest of the house, and begins extraction with truck-mounted or portable pumps. Porous materials that absorbed Category 3 water, carpet padding, drywall below the waterline, insulation, typically get removed rather than dried, since they can't be reliably disinfected. The remaining structure is cleaned with an antimicrobial treatment and dried and monitored the same way any water loss is, following [IICRC standards](https://iicrc.org/iicrcstandards/) for the category of water involved.

## Will homeowners insurance cover this?

Standard homeowners policies usually do not cover sewer or drain backup, or a failed sump pump, unless you've added a water backup endorsement to the policy. That endorsement is often inexpensive relative to the coverage it adds, and it's worth checking your declarations page for it before you assume you're covered either way. The [Pennsylvania Insurance Department](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/insurance/consumer-help-center/learn-about-insurance.html) has consumer resources on how these endorsements work and what to ask your agent. Document the loss with photos before cleanup starts and keep receipts; a written scope of the cleanup work helps either way the claim goes.
