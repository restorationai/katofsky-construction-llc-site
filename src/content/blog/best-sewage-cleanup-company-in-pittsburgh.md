## What should you actually look for in a sewage cleanup company?

Four things separate a company that does this correctly from one that just "cleans up water": contamination-level disinfection, proper removal of unsalvageable materials, a written itemized estimate, and documented disposal.

Sewage backups, whether from a backed-up floor drain, a failed sewer lateral, or a toilet overflow beyond the trap, are classified as Category 3 water, sometimes called black water, under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). That classification isn't a technicality. It determines what gets thrown away instead of dried, what PPE the crew wears, and what disinfection steps are legally and practically required. A company that treats a sewage backup like a clean water leak, drying carpet pad instead of removing it, is cutting a corner that can leave contamination behind the walls.

Before you hire anyone, ask for:

- Proof of IICRC certification and a Pennsylvania contractor license number you can verify
- A written, itemized scope of work before demolition starts, not a verbal estimate
- A plan for which materials get removed versus dried in place, and why
- Documentation of how contaminated debris is bagged, transported, and disposed of

## Is sewage backup covered by homeowners insurance in Pittsburgh?

Most standard homeowners policies do NOT cover sewer or drain backup and sump pump failure unless you've added a water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement; without it, this type of loss is typically excluded.

This is one of the most common, and most expensive, misunderstandings homeowners run into after a backup. Sudden water damage from inside the home, like a burst supply line, is usually covered. A sewer backup is a different category, and insurers generally treat it as an optional add-on, not a default inclusion. Check your declarations page for a backup endorsement before you assume you're covered, and if you're not sure how to read it, [Pennsylvania's Insurance Department](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/insurance/consumer-help-center/learn-about-insurance/) publishes plain-language guidance on what homeowners policies typically include. For background on general coverage patterns, the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance) is a useful starting point too.

Whatever your coverage looks like, document everything: photos before anything is touched, a written scope of work, and receipts for any emergency mitigation. That documentation protects you whether your claim is covered outright or you're paying out of pocket.
