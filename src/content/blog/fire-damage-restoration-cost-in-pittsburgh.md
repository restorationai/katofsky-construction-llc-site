**TL;DR:** Fire damage restoration in Pittsburgh typically costs $1,500 for a single-room smoke cleanup up to $150,000 or more for a total loss requiring full reconstruction. The biggest cost drivers are how far soot and smoke traveled, whether firefighting water soaked the structure, and whether framing, electrical, or roofing need to be rebuilt rather than just cleaned. Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden fire damage, but the deductible and any code-upgrade endorsement affect what you pay out of pocket.

If your kitchen just caught fire from a grease flare-up, or the fire department had to open up a bedroom wall during a larger blaze, you're probably standing in a house that smells like a campfire and wondering what the repair bill looks like before the insurance check even arrives. The range is wide because a contained stove fire and a bedroom-to-attic fire are two completely different jobs. Here's how the pricing actually breaks down, and where the rebuild phase (not just the cleanup) tends to be where the real money goes.

## How Much Does Fire Damage Restoration Cost in Pittsburgh?

Most Pittsburgh homeowners pay somewhere between $1,500 and $150,000 for fire damage restoration, with the median job landing in the $10,000 to $35,000 range for damage contained to one or two rooms. The final number depends almost entirely on how much of the structure needs to be rebuilt versus just cleaned and deodorized.

| Scenario | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Smoke odor only, no direct flame contact (1 room) | $1,500 to $4,000 |
| Minor contained fire (stove or appliance), board-up plus cleaning | $3,000 to $10,000 |
| Moderate damage (1 to 2 rooms, smoke throughout the home) | $10,000 to $35,000 |
| Major damage (multiple rooms or floors, structural rebuild needed) | $35,000 to $80,000 |
| Severe or total loss requiring full reconstruction | $80,000 to $150,000+ |
| Emergency board-up and tarping alone | $300 to $1,500 |

A lot of older Pittsburgh housing stock, the balloon-frame two-stories in Lawrenceville, the hillside homes around Mount Washington, pushes costs toward the higher end once a wall is opened up and plaster, knob-and-tube wiring remnants, or settled framing are involved. Newer construction in the suburbs typically costs less to repair because materials are more standardized and easier to match.

## What Drives the Cost of a Fire Damage Restoration Job?

The single biggest factor is how far the smoke and soot traveled, not how big the flames were. A small fire in an HVAC return can pull soot through an entire house, while a contained stovetop fire with the range hood running might stay in one room.

Other factors that move the price:

- **Material porosity.** Soot embeds into drywall, insulation, and soft goods in a way it doesn't on tile or glass. Porous materials usually get removed and replaced rather than cleaned.
- **Firefighting water.** Most structure fires involve gallons of water from hoses or sprinklers. That water has to be extracted and the space dried before rebuild starts, or mold becomes a second problem within [24 to 48 hours](https://www.epa.gov/mold) on top of the fire.
- **Contents.** Furniture, electronics, and personal items either get cleaned off-site, restored, or written off, and that line item can rival the structural repair cost on a bad loss.
- **Code upgrades.** Pittsburgh and Allegheny County permitting may require bringing an opened-up wall's electrical or framing up to current code, which standard cleaning estimates don't include.
- **Odor removal method.** Light smoke might need a chemical sponge and HVAC filter change. Heavy, deep-set odor often needs thermal fogging or an ozone/hydroxyl treatment cycle, which adds cost and time.

Industry estimates generally put soot and smoke cleaning at roughly $3 to $8 per square foot of affected space, while areas needing a structural rebuild, new drywall, electrical, insulation, and finishes, run $15 to $30 per square foot or more. Debris removal and demolition typically adds $2 to $5 per square foot of material hauled out, or $500 to $3,000 for a single room, since charred debris often needs separate disposal handling.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Fire Damage in Pennsylvania?

Most standard homeowners policies in Pennsylvania cover sudden, accidental fire damage, since fire is one of the named perils in nearly every standard policy. What you pay out of pocket comes down to your deductible and whether your policy includes an ordinance-or-law endorsement that pays for code-required upgrades during the rebuild.

That second point matters more than most homeowners realize. If your home is 40 years old and the fire department opens a wall, the inspector may require updated electrical or framing that your base policy wasn't written to cover. Without that endorsement, the gap between what insurance pays and what code requires comes out of your pocket.

Documentation protects the claim. Photograph the damage before anything is moved, keep every receipt for temporary housing or emergency repairs, and get a written scope of work before demolition starts, not a verbal estimate. The [Pennsylvania Insurance Department](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/insurance/consumer-help-center/learn-about-insurance) has consumer resources on filing a claim and understanding what a standard policy actually covers if you want to check your own policy language before the adjuster walks through.

## What Does the Fire Restoration Process Actually Involve?

Fire restoration happens in a set sequence, and skipping a step is usually what turns a $10,000 job into a $35,000 one.

1. **Emergency board-up and tarping.** Broken windows, a burned-through roof section, or a collapsed wall get secured within hours, not days, to stop weather and theft exposure from adding damage on top of the fire.
2. **Assessment and documentation.** A written scope, photos, and moisture readings get recorded before any demolition, which is what the insurance adjuster will reference.
3. **Water extraction and drying.** If firefighting water soaked the structure, it gets extracted and the space dried, usually with air movers and dehumidifiers, to [IICRC standards](https://iicrc.org/s700/) before anything else happens.
4. **Soot and smoke cleaning.** Non-porous surfaces get cleaned with specialized sponges and solutions; HVAC systems get inspected since soot travels through ductwork.
5. **Demolition of damaged materials.** Charred framing, ruined drywall, and saturated insulation come out. This is where debris removal costs show up.
6. **Odor treatment.** Thermal fogging, ozone, or hydroxyl generators neutralize smoke odor that's worked into porous materials, not just masked it.

For a more detailed walk-through of each phase, see [the full fire damage restoration process](/blog/fire-damage-restoration-process/).

## When Does Fire Damage Require a Full Rebuild, Not Just Cleaning?

A full rebuild becomes necessary once structural framing, roofing, or load-bearing walls are compromised, not just stained with soot. That's the point where the job shifts from a cleaning company's scope into a general contractor's scope.
