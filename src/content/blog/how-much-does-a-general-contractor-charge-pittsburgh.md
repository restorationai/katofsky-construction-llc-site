**TL;DR:** Most general contractors charge one of three ways: 10% to 20% of the total project cost, a cost-plus markup of 15% to 25% over documented materials and labor, or a flat fee for a clearly defined scope. In Pittsburgh, that fee typically covers permits, inspections, subcontractor scheduling, and a workmanship warranty, not just a markup on materials.

If you've got two bids sitting on your kitchen table, one quoting a percentage and one quoting a flat number, and you can't tell which one is actually cheaper, you're not alone. Homeowners in Pittsburgh neighborhoods like Mount Lebanon and Fox Chapel, with their mix of century-old housing stock and hillside lots, often get wider bid spreads than homeowners in newer subdivisions because older homes carry more unknowns. Here's how the fee structures actually work and what a reasonable number looks like for your project size.

## What percentage does a general contractor charge?

Most general contractors charge 10% to 20% of the total project cost when working on a percentage-of-cost basis. That percentage is built into the bottom-line number on your contract, not billed separately.

The percentage covers overhead (insurance, office administration, vehicles), job-site supervision, and profit margin. A simple single-trade job (just a roof, just flooring) tends to land at the lower end. A multi-trade renovation that coordinates a plumber, electrician, and framer on a tight schedule tends to land higher, because coordinating five subcontractors takes more of the GC's time than coordinating one.

| Fee structure | How it's calculated | Typical range |
|---|---|---|
| Percentage-of-cost | % applied to total project cost | 10% to 20% |
| Cost-plus | Documented cost + fixed fee or markup | 15% to 25% markup |
| Fixed-fee / lump sum | One negotiated price for a defined scope | Overhead and profit built into the bid, not itemized |

## General contractor fee vs. cost-plus contract: what's the difference?

A cost-plus contract means you pay the actual, documented cost of labor and materials, plus a fee on top, usually a 15% to 25% markup or a flat dollar amount. A standard percentage-of-cost or fixed-fee contract bundles everything into one number up front.

Cost-plus shows up most often on jobs where the final scope isn't fully known at the start, which is common in reconstruction after fire, storm, or water damage, where hidden framing damage or code-upgrade requirements only surface once walls come open. You get full visibility into what every invoice costs, but the final number can move. A fixed-fee contract gives you price certainty up front but less flexibility if conditions change mid-project.

For a standard kitchen or bathroom remodel with a known scope, a fixed-fee or percentage contract is usually the better fit. For reconstruction tied to an insurance claim, cost-plus with a written, itemized scope is more common because it mirrors how the adjuster's estimate gets built.

## What's included in a general contractor's quote?

A legitimate quote covers more than materials and labor, it covers everything that keeps the job moving and legal. That includes pulling the building permit, scheduling municipal inspections at each phase, sequencing and supervising every subcontractor, procuring materials, and standing behind the finished work with a written workmanship warranty.

If a bid looks unusually low compared to others you've received, ask what's missing. The three most common gaps: no permit fees included, no allowance built in for unforeseen conditions (common in Pittsburgh's older housing stock, where that can mean [asbestos or lead-based materials](/blog/asbestos-lead-abatement/) behind a wall you weren't expecting to open), and no written warranty period.

Pennsylvania also requires contractors doing home improvement work over $5,000 to register with the state as a Home Improvement Contractor. You can confirm a contractor's registration status through the [Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General's HIC registration program](https://www.attorneygeneral.gov/businesses-and-organizations/home-improvement-contractor-registration/) before signing anything.

## Are general contractor estimates free?

A basic walkthrough estimate for a standard remodel is usually free, most reputable contractors in the Pittsburgh market don't charge to come look at a kitchen or bathroom and give you a ballpark range. A detailed, itemized bid that requires drawn plans, engineering review, or a formal scope of work sometimes carries a design fee, which a contractor will typically credit back if you hire them.

Be cautious of a contractor who refuses to put anything in writing until you sign a contract. A written, itemized estimate, even a preliminary one, is the baseline expectation and is what the [National Association of Home Builders](https://www.nahb.org/) recommends homeowners request before comparing bids.

## How much does it cost to renovate a house in Pittsburgh?

Project cost in the Pittsburgh market depends heavily on scope, age of the home, and whether structural or mechanical systems need updating alongside cosmetic work. These are typical industry ranges, not quotes, every project needs its own written scope before work begins.

| Project type | Typical project cost | Typical GC fee included |
|---|---|---|
| Bathroom remodel | $12,000 to $30,000 | $1,500 to $5,000 |
| Kitchen remodel | $25,000 to $60,000 | $3,000 to $9,000 |
| Whole-house renovation | $80,000 to $250,000+ | $10,000 to $40,000+ |
| Fire or water damage reconstruction | Varies by scope, often insurance-paid | Frequently built into the insurance scope rather than billed as a separate percentage |

Older housing stock around hillside neighborhoods and rowhouse blocks close to the city core tends to run toward the higher end of these ranges, because older knob-and-tube wiring, cast iron plumbing, or foundation movement often surface once walls are opened. A contractor who walks the project before quoting, rather than pricing from photos alone, is more likely to catch those conditions early.

## When does a flat fee make more sense than a percentage?

A flat fee makes the most sense when the scope is fully defined before work starts, a single bathroom gut, a roof replacement, a known square footage of flooring. A percentage or cost-plus structure earns its keep on projects where scope can shift, multi-room renovations, additions, or reconstruction tied to storm or fire damage where the full extent isn't visible until demolition begins.
