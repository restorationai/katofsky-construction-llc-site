If a pipe just burst in your Pittsburgh home, go to the main water shutoff first, then make two calls, not one: a licensed plumber to stop the leak at its source, and a restoration company to start pulling out water before it soaks into your walls and floors. Both calls should happen within the same hour if you can manage it. Here's why, and exactly what to do between now and tomorrow morning.

## What do you do in the first 60 seconds?

Shut off the main water valve before you do anything else. In most Pittsburgh-area homes it's near the water meter, often in the basement along the wall closest to the street, or in a utility closet on the ground floor. Turning this valve clockwise stops water flow to the entire house, not just the broken section of pipe.

Next, cut power to any area where water is pooling near outlets, switches, or a breaker panel. If the panel itself is wet or you're not sure, leave it alone and call an electrician instead of guessing. Move anything you can lift away from standing water: rugs, boxes, electronics, furniture legs. If the water is coming from the ceiling, poke a small hole to let it drain in a controlled spot rather than letting pressure build and the ceiling collapse.

Don't run a wet/dry vac in standing water near an outlet, and don't try to repair the pipe yourself unless you're a licensed plumber. A botched DIY fix often makes the insurance conversation harder, not easier.

## Who do you call first, a plumber or a restoration company?

Call both, close together, because they do two different jobs. A licensed plumber repairs or replaces the failed pipe so water stops flowing for good. A restoration company removes standing water, sets up drying equipment, and repairs the damage the water left behind, like soaked drywall, swollen flooring, and insulation.

If a plumber can be on-site within the hour, call them first so the source is fully sealed before extraction starts. If the plumber is backed up, don't wait on them: once the main valve is off, there's no more active leak, so you can call the restoration side first and get drying underway while the plumber gets to you. The reason timing matters is simple. Wet drywall and framing can start growing mold within 24 to 48 hours, according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), so the clock is already running the moment water hits your subfloor.

## What happens in the first 24 hours after a burst pipe?

The first 24 hours are about stopping the damage from spreading, not finishing the repair. A technician arrives, confirms the water has been shut off, and uses a moisture meter to map exactly how far water traveled behind walls and under flooring, often farther than it looks from the surface.

From there, standing water gets extracted with truck-mounted or portable pumps. Wet carpet pad, saturated drywall, and insulation that can't dry in place usually come out, since trapped moisture behind an intact wall is how hidden mold gets started. Commercial air movers and dehumidifiers go in to bring the space down to a normal moisture reading, following the drying principles in the [ANSI/IICRC S500 water damage standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). Full structural drying typically runs three to five days, checked daily with moisture readings, before rebuild work starts.

For a broader look at this phase, [this guide to the first 24 hours after water damage](/blog/what-to-do-first-24-hours-water-damage/) walks through documentation and what to expect room by room.

## Will homeowners insurance cover a burst pipe in Pittsburgh?

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, which is exactly what a burst pipe is. A pipe that fails suddenly, rather than one that's been slowly seeping for months, is the kind of loss insurers typically pay for, per the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance).

What usually isn't covered: damage from a leak that went on for weeks before anyone noticed, or damage tied to poor maintenance. That's one more reason to act fast and document everything: photos of the water line on the walls, the source of the break, and any damaged belongings, before cleanup starts. For more on how this typically plays out, see [does homeowners insurance cover water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/). Call your insurer after the water is shut off and mitigation has started, not before; most adjusters expect you to begin stopping further damage right away.
