When fire damage, sewage backup, or a biohazard scene forces a contractor to tear out drywall, flooring, cabinetry, or framing, someone has to put the structure back together before a family can move back in or a tenant space can reopen. That's the gap Reconstruction Services fills: the rebuild phase that starts after demolition and mitigation are done and ends when the space passes inspection and looks, functions, and is finished the way it was before the loss, or better.

## What Reconstruction Services actually involves

Reconstruction covers everything from a single room of drywall and paint to full structural rebuilds involving framing, subfloor, electrical rough-in coordination, and finish carpentry. In Pittsburgh's older housing stock, that often means matching plaster-and-lath walls instead of drywall, working around balloon-framed structures in pre-1940s homes, and rebuilding in rowhouses and duplexes where shared walls and tight lot lines affect how work gets sequenced. Timelines vary widely: a single damaged room might rebuild in one to two weeks, while a fire-damaged second floor or a sewage-contaminated basement with structural framing loss can run six to ten weeks depending on permitting, material lead times, and the extent of the original loss. Work is performed under Katofsky Construction's license (#090877) and documented at each stage for the homeowner and the insurance carrier.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work.** Before any lumber is ordered, we document existing conditions, measure the affected areas, and write a detailed scope that spells out materials, labor, and sequence. This is the document an adjuster reviews against the estimate.
2. **Permits.** Structural work, electrical, and plumbing tie-ins typically require permits through the City of Pittsburgh or the relevant municipal building department. We pull what's required before framing starts, not after an inspector flags it.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Damaged studs, joists, or subfloor get replaced to match existing structural specs. This is also when rough electrical, plumbing, and HVAC connections are coordinated with licensed trades ahead of drywall.
4. **Drywall, flooring, and finish carpentry.** Walls get hung, taped, and textured to match the surrounding surfaces; flooring is installed or patched; trim, doors, and cabinetry go back in.
5. **Final walkthrough and punch list.** We inspect against the original scope of work, correct anything that doesn't match, and get the space ready for occupancy or reinspection.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common failure point isn't the framing, it's the sequencing. Reconstruction that starts before a space is fully dry or before an affected area has been properly cleared after a sewage or biohazard incident traps the problem behind new drywall, where it resurfaces months later as odor or discoloration. A second common mistake is scope creep that isn't documented: a crew finds additional damage mid-project (rotted subfloor under a charred floor section, for example) and either ignores it or expands the job without updating the written scope, which creates disputes with the insurance carrier later. Adjusters reviewing a reconstruction claim are typically looking for an itemized scope tied to the original damage report, photos at each phase, and a clear line between what was damaged by the covered event versus pre-existing wear. A contractor who documents as they go, rather than reconstructing the paper trail after the fact, moves claims faster and avoids disputes over what should and shouldn't be covered.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Pittsburgh winters affect reconstruction scheduling more than most people expect. Exterior work like roof tie-ins or window replacement tied to a reconstruction project can slow down between December and March, and material deliveries for custom millwork or matching trim in older homes can take longer during that stretch. Humidity swings between seasons also matter for timing drywall and flooring installation, since materials need to acclimate before they're installed in homes with older, less consistent HVAC systems.
