Smoke doesn't stay where the fire was. Within hours of a kitchen fire, a chimney backdraft, or a neighboring structure fire, airborne soot particles work their way into drywall pores, grout lines, HVAC ductwork, and the inside of cabinetry. By the time a homeowner notices the smell lingering days later, the residue has often bonded chemically to surfaces rather than just sitting on top of them. Pittsburgh's older housing stock, full of plaster walls, horsehair lath, and original woodwork, absorbs smoke odor more deeply than modern drywall construction, which is part of why generic cleaning products rarely solve the problem on their own.

## What Smoke Damage Restoration actually involves

Smoke damage restoration is a separate discipline from fire damage repair itself. It focuses specifically on the residue, odor, and corrosive byproducts left behind after flames are extinguished, whether the source was a structure fire, a grease fire, a wood stove malfunction, or smoke infiltration from a nearby event. The work involves identifying the soot type (dry, wet, protein-based, or fuel/oil-based), physically removing residue from every affected surface, and neutralizing odor-causing particles at a molecular level rather than masking them. Depending on the scope, equipment can include HEPA vacuums, dry chemical sponges, thermal foggers, and hydroxyl or ozone generators run in sealed, unoccupied spaces. A single-room job might be done in a day or two; whole-house smoke penetration, especially when it has reached the duct system, typically takes longer.

## Our process

1. **Soot characterization.** We identify whether the residue is dry smoke (fast-burning, powdery, easier to lift) or wet smoke (slow-burning, sticky, often from smoldering synthetic materials), since each requires a different cleaning agent and technique.
2. **Containment and ventilation control.** Affected areas are isolated to stop residue from migrating further into clean rooms, and HVAC systems are shut down or filtered to prevent recirculation.
3. **Surface cleaning.** Dry sponging removes loose particulate first; wet cleaning with pH-balanced solvents follows for bonded residue on walls, ceilings, trim, and hard contents.
4. **Odor neutralization.** Thermal fogging or hydroxyl treatment penetrates the same pathways the smoke used to reach odor particles trapped in porous materials, rather than just treating the surface.
5. **Verification and sealing.** Treated surfaces are inspected, and in cases of deep porous absorption, an odor-blocking sealer is applied before repainting or refinishing.

## What separates a good smoke damage response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating smoke damage like a cleaning job instead of a chemistry problem. Air fresheners and surface wiping can make a room smell fine for a few days before odor resurfaces as temperature and humidity shift. A thorough response identifies every surface the soot reached, including inside supply and return ducts, behind outlet covers, and within cabinet cavities, not just the visibly stained walls. Acidic soot residue from protein-based fires is also corrosive; left untreated on metal fixtures, hinges, and electronics, it can cause pitting and failure weeks after the fire. Insurance adjusters reviewing a claim typically look for documented soot classification, room-by-room photos before and after cleaning, and evidence that odor treatment, not just visible cleaning, was performed. As an IICRC Certified Firm, we document the loss this way as a matter of course, which also tends to speed up claim approval.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Pittsburgh winters bring heavier use of fireplaces, wood stoves, and space heaters, which is reflected in a seasonal uptick in smoke-related calls between November and March. Beyond local fire events, the region has also seen periods of wildfire smoke drifting in from outside the state, which can infiltrate homes through HVAC intake and settle into furniture and fabrics even without a fire on the property. Wildfire smoke cleanup in these cases focuses less on structural soot bonding and more on odor saturation in textiles, carpet, and air systems.
